1 · Why crypto?

Paying online today needs a middleman

  • Online payments go through a trusted intermediary, such as a bank or card network.
  • Payments can be reversed, so the middleman handles disputes. This raises costs and makes tiny payments impractical.
  • Digital files can be copied. So how do you stop someone from spending the same coin twice? This is the double-spending problem.

Analogy (AI-written)

Picture a shared class notebook that everyone has a copy of. If it says "Amy gave Ben 5 coins", everyone sees it, so Amy can't give those same 5 coins to Cara too.

1 · Why crypto?

The big idea: proof instead of trust

"What is needed is an electronic payment system based on cryptographic proof instead of trust"

From the Bitcoin whitepaper, Satoshi Nakamoto (2008), section 1

  • The fix: announce every transaction publicly, and have the network agree on one order. The earliest transaction counts.
  • Bitcoin launched in 2009 with this goal of peer-to-peer, censorship-resistant money.

  • A block is a group of recent transactions. A transaction only counts once it is in a block.
  • A hash is a fingerprint of the data. Each block stores the previous block's hash, and that is what makes the chain.
  • A blockchain is "a public database that is updated and shared across many computers in a network."

  • "The data in a block cannot change without changing all subsequent blocks."
  • Thousands of copies of the ledger sit on computers around the world, which makes it extremely hard to alter.
  • Bitcoin adds a new block roughly every 10 minutes.

3 · Peer-to-peer

No central server: many nodes check each other

The nodes are computers that each keep a copy of the ledger. How a Bitcoin payment spreads:

  1. The transaction is broadcast to all nodes
  2. Nodes collect transactions into a block
  3. Nodes work on the proof-of-work
  4. The winner broadcasts the block
  5. Others accept it only if every transaction is valid and unspent
  6. They build the next block on top of it

Nodes always trust the longest chain. If two blocks appear at the same moment, the tie breaks when the next block arrives.

Traditional payments: a bank validates. Crypto: "a distributed network of nodes validates transactions".

4 · Consensus

How strangers agree on the next block

Proof of Work (Bitcoin)Proof of Stake (Ethereum)
Who adds blocksMiners who find a nonce that makes the block's hash start with enough zerosValidators chosen at random, who lock up (stake) ETH
Hard partHard to find, easy to check (one hash)Other validators check the proposed block
Why stay honestNew coins + fees as reward; rewriting history means redoing all the workRewards for honesty; staked collateral can be destroyed for misbehavior
Voting power"one-CPU-one-vote"Weighted by staked ETH

5 · Bitcoin

The first cryptocurrency

  • Launched in 2009. It is the largest cryptocurrency by market capitalization.
  • It has a hard cap of 21 million coins. New coins come from mining rewards.
  • "We define an electronic coin as a chain of digital signatures." Each owner signs the coin over to the next owner's public key.
  • The public can see amounts moving between keys, but not who owns those keys.

Fun fact

A famous early purchase was two pizzas for 10,000 BTC in 2010.

If you lose your coins or they're stolen, there's limited recourse, because no central authority manages Bitcoin.

5b · Wallets & keys

Your keys are your coins

PUBLIC KEYSafe to share

  • Other people use it to send you crypto. "Think of your public key as your mailing address."
  • Sharing it doesn't let anyone take your money.

PRIVATE KEYNever share

  • It "functions as a password to your crypto wallet". It signs your transactions, and the network won't accept a spend without that signature.
  • Anyone who gets it can take everything in the wallet.

Seed phrase & custody

Many wallets hide the private key behind a seed phrase, a string of random words.

On an exchange wallet (Coinbase, Binance), the exchange holds your key and controls the funds for you.

Lost or stolen keys can't be recovered. Write the phrase down, keep copies in more than one place, and never screenshot or photograph it.

6 · Ethereum

A blockchain with a computer inside

  • Every node keeps the state of one shared computer, called the Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM).
  • ETH is Ethereum's own coin. You pay fees in ETH to run things, and bigger jobs cost more, which stops spam.
  • Smart contracts are programs on the EVM that anyone can call. Apps built from them are called dapps: marketplaces, lending, games.

"you can think of a smart contract like a sort of vending machine"

You put money in and press a button, and the rules run on their own. No cashier is needed.

7 · Stablecoins

Crypto that tries to stay at $1

Normal crypto prices swing a lot, which makes them awkward for everyday payments. Stablecoins are "cryptocurrencies without the volatility."

TypeHow it holds its valueCatch
Fiat-backed (USDT, USDC)1:1 dollars held by an issuerCentralized; reserves need audits
Crypto-backed (DAI/USDS)Over-collateralised, e.g. ≥ $2 of crypto per $1Can lose its peg if the collateral crashes
Commodity-backed (Pax Gold)Backed by goldYou must trust the issuer
AlgorithmicCode grows or shrinks the supplyYou must trust the algorithm

Even Tether can drift from $1 under market stress. The stablecoin market was about $235B as of March 2025.

8 · Exchanges & price

No one "sets" the price: orders meet

  • An order book is "a list of pending buy and sell orders for a cryptocurrency on an exchange."
  • Bids are what buyers will pay. Asks are what sellers want. The gap between them is the spread, and a smaller spread usually means more liquidity.
  • A market order fills right away at the best price. A limit order waits for your price.
  • The exchange keeps matching orders. That continuous matching is price discovery.

Example order book (illustration)

ASK 101.20 × 3
ASK 100.80 × 5
ASK 100.50 × 2
BID 100.30 × 4
BID 100.10 × 6
BID 99.90 × 1

Spread = 100.50 − 100.30 = 0.20. A market buy would take the 100.50 ask. Decentralized exchanges like Uniswap usually use liquidity pools (automated market makers) instead of an order book.

9 · Risks & future

Facts vs. opinions

FACTKnown risks

  • Very volatile, more than the S&P 500. One asset fell more than 50% in a single day.
  • Not legal tender, and no deposit insurance. Exchanges and wallets have been hacked.
  • Little recourse if coins are lost or stolen. Fraud and scandals have happened, such as the ByBit hack.
  • Investors want regulation, but they also fear it could limit crypto's peer-to-peer nature.

OPINIONPredictions (a crypto company's blog)

  • Real-world assets like real estate and stocks will be tokenized.
  • The "Wild West" era is ending as governments regulate.
  • "Stablecoins are crypto's first product-market-fit."

Morgan Stanley's advice: "proceed with caution" and get educated first.

Check yourself

Three quick questions

1. Why can't someone secretly change an old Bitcoin transaction?

Its block's hash would change, which breaks every later block. The attacker would also have to redo the proof-of-work for all of them, faster than the honest network.

2. What is the difference between a fiat-backed and an algorithmic stablecoin?

A fiat-backed coin is backed by dollars an issuer holds, so you trust the issuer. An algorithmic coin uses code to change the supply, so you trust the algorithm.

3. Who decides the price of ETH on an exchange?

Nobody alone. The price comes from bids and asks being matched in the order book, which is called price discovery.
