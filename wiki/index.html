Why crypto? Trust, middlemen and double-spending

In one sentence

Crypto tries to let two people pay each other online without a bank in the middle, by replacing trust in a company with cryptographic proof S1S5.

Key ideas

  • Today, online payments go through a trusted intermediary such as a bank or card network S1S5.
  • Because these payments can be reversed, the middleman has to handle disputes. This raises costs and makes very small payments impractical S1.
  • Digital money has a special problem called double-spending: a file can be copied, so how do you stop someone from spending the same coin twice? S1
  • A central "mint" could check for double-spends, but then the whole system depends on that one company S1.
  • Bitcoin's answer is to announce every transaction publicly and have the network agree on one order of transactions, where the earliest one counts S1.
  • The goal, in the whitepaper's words, is "an electronic payment system based on cryptographic proof instead of trust" S1.
  • Bitcoin started in 2009 with the aim of peer-to-peer, censorship-resistant money S5S7.
  • People use crypto for peer-to-peer value transfer and for smart contracts, for example lending, trading and digital collectibles S5.

Beginner analogy (AI-written, not from a source)

It's like a shared class notebook that everyone has a copy of. If you write "Amy gave Ben 5 coins", everyone sees it, so Amy can't give those same 5 coins to Cara too.

wiki/blockchain-basics.md

Blockchain basics: blocks, hashes, the chain

In one sentence

A blockchain is a public database that is shared and updated across many computers, where data is grouped into blocks and each block points to the one before it S2S5.

Key ideas

  • Block: a group of recent transactions. A transaction only counts once it has been added to a block S2.
  • Hash: a short fingerprint of data. Publishing the hash of a block proves the data existed at that time S1.
  • Chain: each block includes the hash of the previous block, so the blocks link together like a chain S1S2.
  • Changing one old block would change its hash, which breaks every block after it. "The data in a block cannot change without changing all subsequent blocks" S2.
  • Thousands of copies of the ledger are kept on computers around the world, which makes it extremely hard to alter S5.
  • Bitcoin adds a new block roughly every 10 minutes. Other coins have different block times S5.
  • On Ethereum, blocks generally hold dozens to hundreds of transactions S2.

Beginner analogy (AI-written, not from a source)

Think of a stack of numbered receipts where each receipt has the previous receipt's fingerprint printed on it. If you change receipt #3, its fingerprint changes, and receipts #4, #5, #6... no longer match.

wiki/p2p-network.md

Peer-to-peer networks and nodes

In one sentence

Instead of one bank's server, a crypto network is many independent computers (nodes) that each keep a copy of the ledger and check each other's work S2S5.

Key ideas

  • Peer-to-peer (P2P) means the two parties transact directly, with no financial institution in between S1.
  • Nodes are the computers in the network. They must agree on each new block so that everyone has the same data S2.
  • In traditional payments a trusted intermediary validates transactions. In crypto, "a distributed network of nodes validates transactions and records them on a shared blockchain ledger" S5.
  • How a Bitcoin payment spreads through the network, in 6 steps S1:
  • The new transaction is broadcast to all nodes.
  • Nodes collect transactions into a block.
  • Nodes work on the proof-of-work for that block (see consensus).
  • The node that finds it broadcasts the block.
  • Other nodes accept the block only if all of its transactions are valid and not already spent.
  • Nodes show they accept it by building the next block on top of it.
  • Nodes always treat the longest chain as the correct one. If two blocks appear at once, the tie breaks when the next block arrives S1.
  • Light users do not need the full chain. They can keep only block headers and verify their own payment (Simplified Payment Verification) S1.

Beginner analogy (AI-written, not from a source)

It's like a group chat where every member keeps a full copy of the message history. If one person edits their copy, everyone else's copies disagree, so the fake version gets ignored.

wiki/consensus.md

Consensus: Proof of Work vs Proof of Stake

In one sentence

A consensus mechanism is the rule that lets thousands of nodes that don't trust each other agree on which block comes next S2.

Key ideas

  • Blockchains need a consensus mechanism to reach distributed agreement S2.
  • Proof of Work (PoW, used by Bitcoin): nodes search for a number (a nonce) that makes the block's SHA-256 hash start with enough zeros. This is hard to find but takes only one hash to check S1.
  • PoW is "essentially one-CPU-one-vote": influence depends on computing power, not on how many IP addresses you control S1.
  • To change an old block, an attacker would have to redo its work and the work of every later block. Their chance of catching up shrinks exponentially with each new block S1.
  • Bitcoin adjusts the difficulty so that blocks keep arriving at a steady rate S1.
  • Miners are rewarded with new coins plus transaction fees, which gives them a reason to stay honest S1.
  • Proof of Stake (PoS, used by Ethereum): validators lock up (stake) ETH as collateral. Validators are picked at random to propose blocks, and others check them S2.
  • In PoS, staked ETH also weights validators' votes when the network chooses between competing chains (fork choice) S2.
  • In PoS, honest validators are rewarded. The staked ETH is collateral that can be destroyed if a validator misbehaves S2.
  • (opinion/observation) GWI notes that Bitcoin mining has become concentrated in a few large companies with lots of computing power and electricity S6.

Beginner analogy (AI-written, not from a source)

PoW is a lottery where you buy tickets with electricity. PoS is a lottery where you buy tickets by putting down a deposit, and you lose the deposit if you cheat.

wiki/bitcoin.md

Bitcoin (and keys and signatures)

In one sentence

Bitcoin, launched in 2009, was the first cryptocurrency: a peer-to-peer electronic cash system secured by proof-of-work S1S5.

Key ideas

  • Bitcoin is the largest cryptocurrency by market capitalization S5.
  • It has a hard cap of 21 million coins S5.
  • New bitcoins are created by mining. The first transaction in each block gives new coins to the block's creator S1S5.
  • Keys and signatures: "We define an electronic coin as a chain of digital signatures." To pay someone, the owner signs the previous transaction together with the next owner's public key S1.
  • A transaction can combine several inputs and usually has two outputs: the payment and the change sent back to yourself S1.
  • Privacy: the public can see amounts moving between public keys but not who owns them. Using a new key pair for each transaction helps S1.
  • Because no central authority manages it, there is limited recourse if coins are lost or stolen S5.
  • A famous early real-world purchase was two pizzas for 10,000 BTC in 2010 S3S7.

Beginner analogy (AI-written, not from a source)

A public key is like an account number you can share. A digital signature is like signing a cheque: it proves that the owner approved the payment. For how private keys and wallets work, see wallets-and-keys.

wiki/wallets-and-keys.md

Wallets, public keys and private keys

In one sentence

A crypto wallet holds two keys: a public key that others use to send you crypto, and a private key that proves you own it and lets you spend it S8.

Key ideas

  • Public key: used to receive crypto. "Think of your public key as your mailing address." Sharing it does not let anyone take your money S8.
  • Private key: "functions as a password to your crypto wallet and should be kept secret." Anyone who gets it can take all the crypto in that wallet S8.
  • To spend coins, the owner signs the transaction with the private key, and the network can't validate the transaction without that signature S1S8.
  • A Bitcoin coin is "a chain of digital signatures": each payment is signed over to the next owner's public key S1.
  • Many wallets hide the private key behind a seed phrase (secret recovery phrase), a string of random words S8.
  • Custodial vs self-custody: exchange wallets such as Coinbase or Binance hold your private key and control the funds on your behalf S8.
  • A lost or stolen private key can't be recovered S8, and with no central authority there is limited recourse S5.
  • Safety: write the seed phrase down, keep copies in more than one place, and never screenshot or photograph it S8.

Beginner analogy (AI-written, not from a source)

The public key is your mailbox address, which anyone can drop letters in. The private key is the only key that opens the mailbox, and there's no locksmith to call if you lose it.

wiki/ethereum.md

Ethereum and smart contracts

In one sentence

"Ethereum is a blockchain with a computer embedded in it": a shared computer (the EVM) that runs programs called smart contracts S2.

Key ideas

  • Every Ethereum node keeps a copy of the state of one shared computer, the Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM) S2.
  • ETH (ether) is Ethereum's native cryptocurrency. Users pay ETH fees to run transactions. Part of each fee is burned and the rest goes to the validator, and bigger jobs cost more, which prevents spam S2S5.
  • Smart contracts are programs uploaded to the EVM that anyone can call. They work "like a sort of vending machine": when the conditions are met, they act automatically S2.
  • Apps built from smart contracts are called dapps, for example marketplaces, financial tools and games S2.
  • Smart contracts power lending, trading and digital collectibles S5.
  • Ethereum is secured by Proof of Stake (see consensus) S2.
  • Morgan Stanley groups crypto into digital currencies (like Bitcoin), smart-contract platforms (like Ethereum and Solana), and applications S5.

Beginner analogy (AI-written, not from a source)

A vending machine doesn't need a cashier. You insert money and press B4, and the machine follows its rules and gives you the snack. A smart contract does the same with digital assets.

wiki/stablecoins.md

Stablecoins

In one sentence

Stablecoins are crypto tokens designed to stay at a fixed value, usually $1, so you get crypto's speed without its price swings S3S5.

Key ideas

  • They exist because normal crypto prices are volatile, which makes them awkward for everyday payments S3.
  • Fiat-backed (USDT, USDC): each token is backed 1:1 by dollars held by an issuer. Pro: very stable. Con: centralized, and you need audits to trust the reserves S3S5.
  • Crypto-backed (DAI/USDS): backed by more crypto than it is worth, for example at least $2 of crypto for each $1 coin. If the collateral's price falls, the coin can lose its peg S3.
  • Commodity-backed (Pax Gold): backed by gold. You must trust the issuer S3.
  • Algorithmic: no collateral. An algorithm grows or shrinks the supply to hold the price, and you must trust the algorithm S3.
  • Even Tether "can deviate from $1 under market stress" S5.
  • Uses: global transfers, trading pairs on exchanges, and predictable payments S3.
  • Size: the stablecoin market was about $235B as of March 2025 S7. ethereum.org's table lists USDT at about $175B (no as-of date) S3.
  • (opinion) "Stablecoins are crypto's first product-market-fit" S7.

Beginner analogy (AI-written, not from a source)

It's like a casino chip that is always worth exactly $1. You use it because it moves fast inside the system, not because you expect its value to change.

wiki/exchanges-and-price.md

Exchanges and how the price is set

In one sentence

A crypto's price is not set by any company. It comes from buyers and sellers whose orders are matched on exchanges S4.

Key ideas

  • One common way to get crypto is to buy it on an exchange. Others include rewards from some credit cards or shopping sites S5.
  • An order book is "a list of pending buy and sell orders for a cryptocurrency on an exchange" S4.
  • Bids are prices buyers will pay. Asks are prices sellers will accept S4.
  • The spread is the gap between the highest bid and the lowest ask. A smaller spread usually means higher liquidity S4.
  • A market order buys or sells right away at the best available price. A limit order waits for a price you choose S4.
  • When a buy order meets a matching sell order, the exchange matches them automatically. This constant matching is price discovery S4.
  • Slippage happens when your order fills at a worse price than expected, which is common when the market is volatile or the order book is thin S4.
  • Centralized exchanges with order books dominate today. Decentralized exchanges like Uniswap usually use automated market makers, where you trade against a pool of tokens priced by a formula S4.
  • Direct peer-to-peer trading is also possible but is usually less efficient for liquidity S4.
  • Exchanges and wallets have been hacked or have failed in the past, and there is no deposit insurance S5.

Beginner analogy (AI-written, not from a source)

It's like an auction that never closes. Buyers shout their highest offer, sellers shout their lowest price, and a trade happens whenever the two meet.

wiki/risks-and-future.md

Risks and the future

In one sentence

Crypto is very volatile and comes with few safety nets, and its future depends heavily on regulation and adoption, which no one can predict for sure S5S6.

Key ideas

  • Crypto "is characterized by significant volatility", more than the S&P 500. One asset fell more than 50% in a single day S5.
  • Crypto is not legal tender and has no government backing S5.
  • If coins are lost or stolen there is limited recourse, and exchanges and wallets have been hacked S5.
  • Other risks include software bugs, government action, concentration of holdings among a few addresses, fraud, and future threats to encryption such as quantum computing S5.
  • Real scandals and hacks have happened, for example the ByBit hack and the $Libra scandal S7.
  • Investors support regulation because they see it as a way to reduce fraud and volatility. They also fear it could limit crypto's peer-to-peer nature and anonymity S6.
  • Institutions such as banks are entering crypto S6S7.
  • (opinion/prediction) Tokenization of real-world assets, such as real estate and stocks, will grow S7.
  • (opinion/prediction) The "Wild West" phase is ending as governments regulate S7.
  • Morgan Stanley's advice: "proceed with caution" and get educated first S5.

Beginner analogy (AI-written, not from a source)

It's like a new city with no police yet. Exciting things are being built, but if someone steals from you, there may be nobody to call.

title Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System

date 2008 (paper says "as of 2008")
url https://courses.csail.mit.edu/6.857/2015/files/L07-nakamoto-bitcoin-a-peer-to-peer-electronic-cash-system.pdf

added_by Claude (proposed), Pure approved source list
type primary source

Notes (paraphrased by section)

  • Abstract: P2P electronic payments without a financial institution; double-spending solved by timestamping transactions into a chain of hash-based proof-of-work; secure while honest nodes control most CPU power.
  • §1: Online payments rely on trusted third parties (financial institutions). Reversible payments need mediation → higher costs, no small payments, merchants wary. Goal: cryptographic proof instead of trust, so two parties transact directly.
  • §2: A coin is a chain of digital signatures: each owner signs a hash of the previous transaction + next owner's public key. Payee can't tell if an earlier owner double-spent; a central mint could check but then everything depends on it. Fix: announce transactions publicly and agree on one order; earliest counts.
  • §3: Timestamp server: hash a block of items and publish; each timestamp includes the previous hash, forming a chain.
  • §4: Proof-of-work (like Hashcash): find a nonce so SHA-256 hash starts with N zero bits; hard to find, one hash to verify. Changing a block means redoing it and all later blocks. "One-CPU-one-vote"; longest chain = most work. Difficulty adjusts to target a set number of blocks per hour.
  • §5 Network steps: broadcast tx → nodes collect into block → find PoW → broadcast block → accept only if all tx valid and unspent → build next block on its hash. Longest chain is correct; ties break when next block arrives.
  • §6 Incentive: first tx in a block creates new coins for the block creator; plus transaction fees. Greedy attacker earns more by following rules.
  • §7–9: Merkle tree pruning; header ~80 bytes, ~4.2 MB/year at 1 block / 10 min; SPV light clients; tx have multiple inputs/outputs (payment + change).
  • §10 Privacy: public keys are anonymous; public sees amounts moving between keys, not identities. New key pair per tx.
  • §11: Attacker can only try to reverse his own recent spend; cannot create value or steal. Catch-up probability drops exponentially with confirmations z (e.g. q=0.10 → z=5 for P<0.1%).

Quotes

  • §1 "What is needed is an electronic payment system based on cryptographic proof instead of trust"
  • §2 "We define an electronic coin as a chain of digital signatures."
  • §4 "Proof-of-work is essentially one-CPU-one-vote."
  • §5 "Nodes always consider the longest chain to be the correct one and will keep working on extending it."
  • §11 "An attacker can only try to change one of his own transactions to take back money he recently spent."

Notes

  • Blockchain = a public database updated and shared across many computers. "Block": data stored in consecutive groups; a tx succeeds only once added to a block. "Chain": each block cryptographically references its parent; changing a block requires changing all later blocks and network consensus.
  • Nodes must agree on each new block → consensus mechanism needed.
  • Ethereum uses proof-of-stake: block producers stake ETH as collateral and run validator software; validators are randomly selected to propose blocks; others check; rewards and penalties encourage honesty.
  • Ethereum = "a blockchain with a computer embedded in it": a single canonical computer, the EVM; every node keeps its state.
  • ETH is the native cryptocurrency; requests pay an ETH fee ("bounty"), part burned, rest to the worker; scales with resources; prevents spam. ETH also secures the network (rewards, slashable collateral, vote weight). (Page does not use the word "gas".)
  • Smart contracts: reusable programs uploaded to the EVM, called with parameters; "like a sort of vending machine". Anyone can publish/call for a fee. Apps on them = dapps (marketplaces, financial instruments, games).
  • Accounts hold ETH; a transaction = a fulfilled request + its state change.

  • "The data in a block cannot change without changing all subsequent blocks"
  • "Ethereum is a blockchain with a computer embedded in it."

  • Algorithmic: no collateral; algorithm expands/shrinks supply to hold target price. − must trust the algorithm; balance changes.
  • Uses: global transfers, trading pairs, predictable payments, lending for interest.
  • Market table (no as-of date): USDT ≈ $174.6B, USDC ≈ $68.4B market cap. Time-sensitive.

Notes

  • An order book = list of pending buy and sell orders on an exchange; used to match orders and determine market prices.

type crypto news / education article

Notes

  • A crypto wallet comes with two keys, public and private, that do complementary jobs.
  • Public key: used to receive crypto; like a mailing address or account number that anyone can know. Sharing it does not let anyone withdraw your money.
  • Private key: proves ownership. The wallet needs it to "sign" transactions that spend your funds, and the network can't validate a transaction without it.
  • Private key = "a password to your crypto wallet", so keep it secret. Anyone who gets it can take all the crypto in that wallet.
  • Private keys are numbers. Wallets often hide them behind a seed phrase ("secret recovery phrase"), which is a string of random words (example: MetaMask).
  • Exchange wallets and custodians (e.g. Coinbase, Binance) hold the private key for you and control the funds on your behalf.
  • Safety: write the key or seed phrase down and keep copies in several places. Don't screenshot or photograph it, because hackers target those copies.
  • If the key is lost or stolen, there is "no way to recover it".
  • ⚠ CLAIM FLAGGED AT INGEST: "The public key encrypts transactions, which can be decrypted only by the corresponding private key". This conflicts with S1, where transactions are signed with digital signatures, not encrypted. DO NOT USE.
  • Not covered: how an address is derived, wallet types (hardware/software), seed phrase length, custody pros/cons.

Quotes

  • "Think of your public key as your mailing address."
  • "The private key functions as a password to your crypto wallet and should be kept secret."
  • "Don't take a screenshot of it or take a picture with your phone as these digital copies are often targeted by hackers."
